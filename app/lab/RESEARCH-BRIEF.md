## PART 1 — NEW STATION CANDIDATES (15 ideas, ranked roughly by fit for async digital)

### 1. THE STRESS-REDUCING CONVERSATION (Gottman)

**Framework / source.** John & Julie Gottman, *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work* (2000); part of the “Six Magic Hours” architecture and the daily reunion ritual.
**Evidence base.** Built on Gottman’s longitudinal “Love Lab” observational studies; correlated with positive sentiment override and the 5:1 positive-to-negative interaction ratio that distinguishes “masters” from “disasters.”  It’s worth flagging that Gottman’s specific numerical claims (94%/96% divorce prediction)  have been credibly criticized — Heyman & Slep (2001) showed that several of those figures were *postdiction* on the same dataset rather than true cross-validated prediction; when crossvalidated on independent samples, accuracy dropped substantially.  The underlying behavioral patterns Gottman identified (Four Horsemen, harsh startup, repair attempts) replicate reasonably well; the precise accuracy numbers don’t. The Stress-Reducing Conversation itself is a low-claim intervention — it’s just structured supportive listening — so the evidence-base concern is minimal.
**Mechanic (async).** One partner names an *external* stressor (work, family-of-origin, health — explicitly NOT something between the two of you). They write three things: the situation, the feeling, what they need (vent/advice/distraction/affection). The other writes back following six rules drawn directly from Gottman: (1) show genuine interest; (2) communicate understanding; (3) take their side; (4) “we against the world” framing;  (5) express affection; (6) validate emotions. *No advice unless explicitly requested.* The reveal shows side-by-side: the stressor + the response, plus a slider where the original sender rates “I felt heard” 1–10.
**Mechanism.** Co-regulation through felt understanding; deposits to the “emotional bank account”;  protects the relationship from being where stress is *stored* by making it the place where stress is *metabolized*.
**Contraindications.** This exercise is explicitly about external stress only.  The Gottmans are clear: do not try to use this format for relationship grievances  — it collapses into defensiveness. Build a separate “State of the Union” channel for that.

### 2. STATE OF THE UNION (Gottman)

**Framework / source.** Gottman’s added 6th Magic Hour (post-2000 cultural-shift addition). 
**Evidence base.** Same Gottman caveats as above. The structure is psychoeducation; the content of the structure (5 appreciations → concerns using gentle startup → repair attempt → planning) is directly drawn from the Sound Relationship House levels.
**Mechanic (async).** A weekly ritual with four locked steps: (a) each partner writes 5 specific appreciations from the past week  (must include behavior + meaning, e.g., “When you brought me coffee Tuesday I felt cared about because…”); (b) each names ONE concern using Gottman’s gentle startup formula: *“I feel ___ about ___ (situational, not character) and I need ___”*  (no “you always,” no “you never,” no character indictments); (c) each proposes one repair / behavior change request; (d) each writes one thing to look forward to together. Reveal shows them paired.
**Mechanism.** Prevents Gottman’s “negative sentiment override” by maintaining the 5:1 ratio; makes the gentle-startup the only legal way to register a complaint; turns conflict into a scheduled, contained channel.
**Contraindications.** If contempt is currently active, do this with a therapist first. Gentle startup fails when one partner is in fight/flight.

### 3. HOLD ME TIGHT — ATTACHMENT PROTEST DIALOGUE (EFT / Sue Johnson)

**Framework / source.** Sue Johnson, *Hold Me Tight* (2008); the educational adaptation of Emotionally Focused Therapy.
**Evidence base.** EFT is one of the most empirically-supported couples therapies in existence. Multiple meta-analyses show large effect sizes, with ~70–75% of couples moving from distressed to non-distressed and ~90% showing significant improvement. The Attachment Injury Resolution Model (Makinen & Johnson, 2006; 3-year follow-up by Halchuk, Makinen & Johnson, 2010) shows maintained gains in dyadic adjustment, trust, and forgiveness. There’s also an fMRI study (Johnson et al., 2013) showing EFT changes how partner contact mediates threat response in the brain.
**Mechanic (async).** Frame: “What’s your raw spot?” Partners separately answer, in this order:

1. *The trigger:* “When you ___, I…”
1. *The reactive feeling I show on the surface:* (anger, withdrawal, criticism)
1. *The softer feeling underneath:* (fear, shame, loneliness, feeling unimportant)
1. *The attachment fear:* “I started to wonder if I was [unwanted / too much / not enough / alone].”
1. *The longing:* “What I wished for in that moment was…”
   The reveal pairs them, then asks each partner to respond with a single sentence of “accessibility, responsiveness, engagement” (the ARE framing in EFT) — *not* problem-solving, just acknowledgment of the fear and the longing.
   **Mechanism.** EFT’s core change event is the “softening” — when a withdrawer reaches and a pursuer asks vulnerably for what they need, attachment bond reorganizes. Async lets the softer underneath layer surface without the in-room reactive overlay.
   **Contraindications.** Active affair (untreated), domestic violence, or active addiction — EFT’s stance is that safety must be stabilized first. Also: an unwilling-to-engage partner kills this. If one person writes only at the surface layer and refuses to go to the soft feeling, don’t force it.

### 4. IFS PARTS DIALOGUE FOR COUPLES

**Framework / source.** Richard Schwartz, Internal Family Systems;  couples adaptation in *You Are the One You’ve Been Waiting For* (2008) and Toni Herbine-Blank’s *Intimacy from the Inside Out* (IFIO).
**Evidence base.** IFS was added to the SAMHSA National Registry of Evidence-Based Programs in 2015.  A 2025 scoping review (Taylor & Francis) catalogued 27 studies — mostly case studies and quasi-experimental, with two RCTs.  The evidence is weaker than EFT or Gottman methodologically, but face validity is high and clinical reports of couple use are strong.
**Mechanic (async).** Each partner answers three questions when a recent fight comes up: (1) “Which part of me showed up? (Manager — controller / pleaser / critic; Firefighter — withdrawer / numb-out / rage; Exile — the young hurt one underneath).”  (2) “What is that part trying to protect me from?” (3) “What part of you do I think it was talking to?” Reveal shows the parts diagram side-by-side. Optional layer: each writes a brief “Self-led” message to the other’s exile.
**Mechanism.** Externalizes internal conflict — the fight is reframed as Manager-to-Manager rather than Person-vs.-Person; reduces blame,  increases self-compassion and curiosity (“the 8 C’s of Self”: curiosity, calm, clarity, connectedness, confidence, courage, creativity, compassion).
**Contraindications.** Complex trauma without a therapist. Schwartz himself is cautious about unburdening exile parts outside therapy — keep the station at the *mapping/identification* level, not the *unburdening* level. Don’t have couples speak directly to each other’s exiles in writing without containment.

### 5. NVC — OBSERVATION / FEELING / NEED / REQUEST

**Framework / source.** Marshall Rosenberg, *Nonviolent Communication* (2003).
**Evidence base.** NVC has a thinner controlled-trial base than Gottman or EFT — it’s more practitioner-validated than RCT-validated. Bonnell, Li & van Lingen (2017)  showed positive organizational outcomes; the framework’s logic is consonant with broader research on “I-statements,” need-naming, and reducing reactive defensiveness.
**Mechanic (async).** A four-field form for each partner. Strict rules per Rosenberg:

- **Observation:** what a video camera would record. No “always,” “never,” “you’re being.” (“On Tuesday at dinner, when I said X, you looked at your phone for about a minute.”) 
- **Feeling:** from a curated emotion-word menu (NVC has a canonical needs/feelings list). No “I feel that you…” — that’s a thought, not a feeling.
- **Need:** universal human needs — connection, autonomy, rest, contribution, meaning, etc.  Not strategies. (“I need to feel important to you,” not “I need you to look at me when I talk.”)
- **Request:** specific, present-tense, doable, refusable.  (“Would you be willing to put your phone face-down during dinner this week?”)
  Reveal pairs both completed forms. Receiver responds with: “Are you saying [reflection]? And what you needed was [need-naming]?”
  **Mechanism.** Separates observation from evaluation (the cognitive move that prevents most defensiveness); names need rather than strategy (needs are universal, strategies are negotiable).
  **Contraindications.** Don’t use under flooding. NVC works at HRV-baseline; under threat, the four steps collapse into pseudo-NVC (“I feel that you are an asshole” is not NVC). Build in a flood-check.

### 6. THE ATTACHMENT MAP (ECR-R Short Form + Shared Reveal)

**Framework / source.** Brennan, Clark & Shaver’s Experiences in Close Relationships scale, revised (Fraley, Waller & Brennan, 2000); the gold standard self-report measure of adult romantic attachment on two dimensions: anxiety and avoidance. 
**Evidence base.** ECR-R has hundreds of validation studies. Wei et al.‘s 12-item ECR-S (2007),  the ECR-RD12 in primary care (Ehrenthal et al., 2017), and the recent 10-item ECR-R-10 (2023)  all show good psychometrics. *The classification language (“anxious/avoidant/secure”) is a simplification — the actual model is dimensional.*
**Mechanic (async).** Each partner takes the 12-item ECR-S (~2 min). The reveal plots both partners on a 2-D plane (anxiety × avoidance) with the four quadrants visible. Below the plot: prompts tailored to each combination — e.g., anxious + avoidant pairing gets “the pursuer-distancer cycle” psychoed; secure + anxious gets “your secure partner is a secure base — what would help you let yourself be soothed?” Crucially, frame as *patterns under stress*, not personality types.
**Mechanism.** Naming a pattern depersonalizes it. “We’re in a pursue-withdraw cycle” beats “you never talk / you’re so needy.”
**Contraindications.** Don’t let this become a fixed identity (“I’m avoidant, that’s just who I am”). Attachment is malleable — research on “earned secure” attachment shows strategies and internal working models change in response to corrective relational experiences. Frame results as a snapshot.

### 7. THE 36 QUESTIONS (Aron — adapted)

**Framework / source.** Arthur Aron, Edward Melinat et al. (1997), *PSPB*, “The Experimental Generation of Interpersonal Closeness.”
**Evidence base.** The original study showed that escalating reciprocal self-disclosure with sustained mutual eye contact created lab-measurable increases in closeness in 45 minutes among strangers. Subsequent replications: closeness gains in cross-race pairs (reduced biomarkers of stress, Page-Gould lab); reductions in prejudice (Hungary, Roma study); used in Aron’s later “self-expansion” research with established couples to rekindle novelty-driven closeness. Note Elaine Aron’s own caveat: this was *not* designed to make couples fall in love and was not validated longitudinally.
**Mechanic (async).** Three escalating sets of 12 questions. Both partners must answer each before the next reveals. Reveal is paired side-by-side. End with the four-minute eye contact exercise (recommend doing in person at end). For long-term couples, swap the original questions for the *Self-Expansion Questions* Aron developed for established couples (novelty/discovery questions like “What’s something you’ve changed your mind about in the past year that I might not know?”).
**Mechanism.** “Sustained, escalating, reciprocal, personal self-disclosure” paired with mutual perception of being liked. Self-expansion theory (Aron & Aron) says couples grow closer when they include each other’s experiences and identities in the self.
**Contraindications.** Long-distance / long-tenure couples may find original Set 1 too getting-to-know-you. None clinically.

### 8. APPRECIATION RITUAL — find/remind/bind (Algoe)

**Framework / source.** Sara Algoe’s “find, remind, and bind” theory of gratitude (Algoe, 2012, *Social and Personality Psychology Compass*).  
**Evidence base.** Algoe and Fredrickson’s experimental work shows gratitude expressions function relationally (not just hedonically) — they signal communal-norm engagement, prompt *upward spirals* of mutual responsiveness,  and predict relationship maintenance. Algoe et al.‘s research on “other-praising behavior” (PMC4988174) found that gratitude that names the *partner’s qualities* (not just the act or one’s own benefit) is the active relational ingredient. Lambert et al. (2010) found expressing gratitude to a partner increased the expresser’s perception of communal strength in the relationship.
**Mechanic (async).** Daily or weekly. Each partner writes one appreciation in this strict three-part form per Algoe’s research:

1. *Specific behavior:* what they actually did
1. *Cost or effort to them:* what it took
1. *What it revealed about who they are* (the “other-praising” element — this is the ingredient that makes it stick)
   Reveal pairs them. Optionally, surface a “gratitude streak” counter.
   **Mechanism.** Other-praising naming activates partner’s perception of being seen-for-who-they-are, which is what binds. Generic “thanks for X” doesn’t have the same effect — Algoe’s data is specific on this.
   **Contraindications.** None for couples in repair. For couples in active rupture, premature gratitude can feel like papering over.

### 9. REACH FORGIVENESS PROTOCOL

**Framework / source.** Everett Worthington, *Forgiving and Reconciling* (2003); REACH = Recall, Empathize, Altruistic gift, Commit, Hold on. 
**Evidence base.** Wade, Hoyt, Kidwell & Worthington (2014) meta-analysis of 54 studies in *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* found significant effects (d ≈ 0.56 vs. no-treatment) for forgiveness interventions. The 2024 narrative review (Worthington, *Int J Group Psychother*) of 24 REACH studies found mean d/hr = 0.089.  REACH and Enright’s process model are roughly equivalent;  both work better with longer dose. Worth flagging: Wade et al.’s meta-analysis found REACH was *less* effective with couple groups than with individuals — the relational complexity often needs more than psychoeducation.
**Mechanic (async).** Five sequential pages, the offended partner working through each:

1. **R**ecall the hurt — write what happened, factual
1. **E**mpathize — write a paragraph from the offender’s perspective; what might have been going on for them (this is the hardest step and the most predictive of forgiveness)
1. **A**ltruistic gift — recall a time you were forgiven; consider gifting forgiveness now
1. **C**ommit — write a “forgiveness statement” you can return to
1. **H**old on — name the cue that will trigger doubt and your plan for it
   The offending partner does a parallel apology track (see #10).
   **Mechanism.** Reduces unforgiveness (rumination + revenge motivation + avoidance); the empathy step is doing the bulk of the work mechanistically.
   **Contraindications.** *Critical:* forgiveness is intrapersonal, reconciliation is interpersonal — they’re not the same. Don’t frame this as obligating reconciliation. Also: NOT for ongoing harm — Worthington is explicit that forgiveness work pre-supposes the offense has stopped. Not for active abuse, active affairs, or current harm. With trauma-rooted injuries (attachment injuries in EFT terms), this needs therapist containment.

### 10. THE APOLOGY ARCHITECTURE

**Framework / source.** Distillation of Gottman’s repair attempts research, Lazare’s *On Apology* (2004), and Schumann’s research on what makes apologies land.
**Evidence base.** Schumann (2018, *Curr Dir Psych Sci*) catalogues 8 elements of comprehensive apologies; effective apologies contain more elements. Gottman’s repair attempt literature shows that effective repair predicts marital stability above and beyond the four horsemen (couples can have all four horsemen and still be stable if they repair effectively — 84% of newlyweds high on horsemen but high on repair were stable 6 years later). 
**Mechanic (async).** Eight prompts the apologizing partner answers separately, with the option to omit any but seeing what they omitted:

1. Specific naming of what was done
1. Acknowledgment of impact (“I see that you felt ___”)
1. Acceptance of responsibility (no “but,” no “you also”)
1. What the *partner* lost or had to carry
1. What was happening in *me* that’s an explanation, not an excuse
1. What I will do differently and how you’ll be able to tell
1. What I’m asking of you (nothing? time? a conversation?)
1. The repair offer
   Receiver then rates each element on a “this landed / didn’t land” scale.
   **Mechanism.** Most apologies fail on elements 4 and 6 (impact + behavior change). Forcing differentiation between explanation and excuse is the linchpin.
   **Contraindications.** Don’t deploy this in betrayal trauma without therapeutic containment — the receiver may dissociate or flood and need to pause.

### 11. SHARED MEANING / RITUALS OF CONNECTION (Gottman SRH Level 7)

**Framework / source.** Gottman’s Sound Relationship House top floor: Shared Meaning System.
**Evidence base.** Gottman’s research on rituals of connection in “masters” couples; broader research on ritual in close relationships (Fiese et al., on family rituals and adjustment).
**Mechanic (async).** A four-field shared canvas:

- **Rituals** (formal: anniversaries; informal: how we say goodbye)
- **Roles** (what does it mean to be a partner / parent / sibling / friend in our family?)
- **Goals** (1-year, 5-year, lifetime — narrative, not metric)
- **Symbols** (objects, places, songs, words that mean us)
  Each partner fills privately. Reveal shows overlap (yes — we share this) and gap (only one of us named this — opportunity to discuss).
  **Mechanism.** Builds the “we-narrative” that predicts long-term satisfaction (Buehlman, Gottman & Katz 1992 oral history coding work).
  **Contraindications.** None.

### 12. DIFFERENTIATION CHECK-IN (Schnarch / Crucible)

**Framework / source.** David Schnarch, *Passionate Marriage* (1997) and *Intimacy & Desire* (2009); Schnarch & Regas (2012, *Journal of Marital and Family Therapy*) validated the 63-item Crucible Differentiation Scale on 4,169 participants.  The “Four Points of Balance” are: (1) Solid Flexible Self, (2) Quiet Mind / Calm Heart, (3) Grounded Responding, (4) Meaningful Endurance. 
**Evidence base.** The CDS itself is psychometrically validated; Schnarch’s broader claims (including the controversial “sexual crucible” model that desire decline in long-term couples is a differentiation problem rather than a familiarity problem) are theoretically rich but less empirically tested in RCTs. He sits in tension with Sue Johnson — Schnarch emphasizes self-soothing and “holding onto yourself,” Johnson emphasizes co-regulation and reaching for the other. Both are partially right; couples need both.
**Mechanic (async).** Four questions, separately:

1. *Solid self:* “What’s a value of mine I held onto this week even when it would have been easier not to?”
1. *Quiet mind:* “When I was activated this week, did I self-soothe before reaching out, or did I reach out *to be* soothed? What did that look like?”
1. *Grounded responding:* “Was there a moment I responded from my best self (not reactively)? Tell the story.”
1. *Meaningful endurance:* “What discomfort am I tolerating in service of my own growth right now?”
   Reveal shows both. Optional: shared reflection on “where did we co-regulate well, where did one of us need to hold our own?”
   **Mechanism.** Schnarch’s claim is that low differentiation makes intimacy claustrophobic and desire collapse;  building self-validated intimacy creates space for eros. Whether or not the desire claim holds (debatable), the self-regulation work is solid.
   **Contraindications.** Schnarch’s tone in his own writing is famously confrontational. In a station, frame gently. Don’t do this if one partner is being asked to “hold their own” through what is actually emotional neglect — differentiation language can be weaponized.

### 13. EROTIC SELF / SEXUAL CANDOR (Perel) + YES/NO/MAYBE

**Framework / source.** Esther Perel, *Mating in Captivity* (2006);  the yes/no/maybe list comes out of the BDSM communities  and was adopted by  AASECT-certified sex therapists  as a structured communication tool.
**Evidence base.** Perel’s writing is clinical observation, not RCT — she’s a synthesizer not an empiricist. The underlying claims have research support: MacNeil & Byers (2009) and Mark & Jozkowski (2013) on sexual communication and sexual satisfaction; sexual self-disclosure predicts sexual and relationship satisfaction. Yes/no/maybe lists don’t have efficacy trials but are widely used in  AASECT sex therapy. 
**Mechanic (async).** Two parts:
**Part A — Erotic self-disclosure (Perel-style prompts):**

- “I am most turned on when I feel ___”
- “Something I want that I’ve never said is ___”
- “What I learned about sex/desire growing up was ___”
- “In our sex life I feel most like myself when ___”
- “Something I’m curious about is ___”
- “Something that brings me out of the moment is ___”
  **Part B — Yes/No/Maybe** (curated, tasteful list — partners check independently). Reveal shows overlap on “yes/yes” and “maybe/yes” zones (the conversation starters), with hard “no” boundaries protected.
  **Mechanism.** Perel’s core argument:  desire requires space,  otherness, and the ability to *want* (which requires a sovereign self that feels worthy of receiving). Sexual candor — the ability to talk about it — is the prerequisite for desire to evolve.
  **Contraindications.** Significant: not for couples in active sexual coercion, betrayal trauma, or where one partner has untreated trauma that may surface. The yes/no/maybe specifically can be triggering if there’s history. Frame as “this is for couples already in a basically safe sexual rapport who want more language.”

### 14. BODY MAP — WHERE I FEEL THIS (Somatic / Polyvagal-informed)

**Framework / source.** Nummenmaa et al. (2014), *PNAS*, “Bodily maps of emotions” — established that emotions have reliable somatotopic patterns across cultures. Combined with somatic experiencing (Levine) and interoception research (Craig, Critchley).
**Evidence base.** Nummenmaa’s body-maps paper has been replicated across cultures; the underlying claim that emotions are felt in specific bodily regions and that interoceptive awareness is trainable is well-supported. Khalsa et al. (2018) review of interoception in mental health is the canonical source.
**Mechanic (async).** Each partner gets an outline of a body. A current emotion-word is selected (or “what I’m carrying right now”). They paint/highlight the regions where they feel it — color-coded for quality (warm/cool, expanding/contracting, dense/diffuse). Add a one-sentence somatic description: “It feels like ___.” Reveal shows both bodies side-by-side.
**Mechanism.** Builds interoceptive accuracy (associated with emotion regulation), externalizes affect into shared visual language, gives partner a non-verbal entry point into your state.
**Contraindications.** For partners with significant trauma or dissociation, body-focus can be destabilizing. Offer an opt-out and frame as “as much or as little as feels okay.”

### 15. VALUES CARD SORT (ACT)

**Framework / source.** Acceptance and Commitment Therapy values work (Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson, 1999); also overlaps with Miller & Rollnick’s Motivational Interviewing values card sort.
**Evidence base.** ACT has hundreds of RCTs across conditions. Values clarification specifically is associated with committed action and meaning-making. The card sort is a standard ACT clinical procedure.
**Mechanic (async).** Each partner sorts ~50 value words into “very important / important / not important” then forces a top 5. Reveal shows both top 5s, plus a “shared values” intersection and a “different values” zone with a prompt: “Different values aren’t a problem to solve. They’re a shape to live with. Where do these meet? Where do they create friction we can name?”
**Mechanism.** Surfaces what each is actually navigating *toward*; reframes perpetual conflicts (Gottman: 69% of couple conflicts are perpetual) as values differences to be honored rather than problems to be solved.
**Contraindications.** None.

### Bonus / shorter ones worth considering:

**16. FUTURE-SELF LETTER.** Each partner writes a letter from their 80-year-old self to themselves and their partner today. Draws on Hershfield’s research on future-self continuity (NeuroImage, 2009) — increased future-self continuity predicts better long-term decision-making. Adapted for couples it surfaces what you want this relationship to have *been*.

**17. EMOTION GRANULARITY GAME.** Lisa Feldman Barrett’s research shows higher emotional granularity (using more precise emotion words) predicts better regulation, less alcohol misuse, less aggression,  less neural reactivity to rejection (Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight, 2015, *Curr Dir Psych Sci*).  Mechanic: each partner names the precise emotion-word for a recent moment from a 100-word menu organized by valence × arousal, justifies it, then guesses the partner’s word. Reveal shows match/mismatch and the reasoning.

**18. THE HIDDEN DREAM (Gottman gridlock work).** For perpetual problems: each partner writes the *dream within the conflict* — the deeper hope, fear, value, or family-of-origin pattern underneath the surface position. Reveal shows the dreams. The conversation pivots from positions to dreams.

**19. CONFLICT STYLE ID + DE-ESCALATION SIGNAL.** Quick assessment of each partner’s stress style (Gottman’s typology: validating / volatile / conflict-avoiding) + Stan Tatkin’s “couple bubble” cues. They co-create a *de-escalation signal* — a word, gesture, or written cue — they will both honor. Tatkin’s *Wired for Love* draws on attachment + Schore’s affect regulation work.

**20. GRIEF / LOSS RITUAL.** For couples carrying a shared loss (miscarriage, parent’s death, lost dream). Draws on Worden’s tasks of mourning and Bonanno’s grief research. Each writes: what was lost, what changed, what we’re carrying, what we want to make of it. Reveal pairs them, then they co-author one small ritual.

## PART 2 — DEEPENING THE FOUR EXISTING STATIONS

### A) RESONANCE — what the science actually says about frequency, sound, and the body

**Be careful here. This is where the popular wellness literature is most contaminated by pseudoscience.**

**Solfeggio frequencies are not validated.** This is unambiguous. The claim that 528 Hz repairs DNA, 396 Hz “releases guilt,” etc., traces to Joseph Puleo and Leonard Horowitz (1999, *Healing Codes for the Biological Apocalypse*) — they derived the numbers via Pythagorean numerology from the Latin hymn *Ut queant laxis*. Science Feedback (2021) and RationalWiki both classify the claims as pseudoscience.  There is no plausible mechanism by which a specific Hz value (as opposed to a *range*, a *waveform pattern*, or a *contextual cue*) would have specific health effects. The Tesla quote often invoked (“If you want to find the secrets of the universe, think in terms of energy, frequency, and vibration”) has no documented source. Some studies on 528 Hz exist (e.g., the cellular work cited on MindEasy) but they’re small, methodologically weak, and don’t replicate.

**Vibroacoustic therapy is real but does not validate Solfeggio claims.** VAT applies low-frequency vibration (typically 30–120 Hz) directly to the body and has shown modest effects on pain, anxiety, and muscle tension in clinical trials (e.g., Naghdi et al. on fibromyalgia). This is a *tactile-acoustic* intervention, not a tonal-frequency-as-information intervention. Don’t conflate them.

**What is real about sound and the nervous system:**

1. **Prosody and the social engagement system.** Stephen Porges’s polyvagal work — and this is the part that’s empirically grounded, distinct from the broader popular polyvagal narrative which is more theoretical than tested — shows that the middle ear muscles (stapedius, tensor tympani), facial expression, and vocal prosody share a neuroanatomical circuit through cranial nerves V, VII, IX, X, and XI. Mid-frequency, melodic, modulated human voice (roughly the range of human female voice with prosodic variation, approximately 300–3000 Hz) preferentially activates this circuit and signals safety. Flat, monotonal, low-frequency, or harsh-onset sounds can do the opposite. **This is the part of polyvagal theory that has empirical support; the broader “ladder” model of ventral/sympathetic/dorsal as discrete autonomic states is more clinically useful metaphor than validated neuroscience.** Paul Grossman has published critiques of polyvagal theory’s specific neuroanatomical claims (Grossman & Taylor, 2007; Grossman, 2023). Be honest about this with Tamara.
1. **The Safe and Sound Protocol** (Porges) — 5-day filtered acoustic intervention based on this prosody work — has small studies in autism (Porges et al., 2013) but the broader evidence base is thin.
1. **Lower frequencies (40–80 Hz)** in vibroacoustic therapy show physiological effects (parasympathetic activation, muscle relaxation) when delivered tactilely. Audibly, lower frequencies tend to be experienced as grounding/heavy/inward; higher frequencies as activating/alert/outward. This is consistent with general psychoacoustic research (Juslin & Västfjäll, 2008, on music and emotion).
1. **Binaural beats** — the popular literature massively overclaims. A 2023 meta-analysis (Lopez-Caballero et al.) found small but statistically detectable effects on anxiety; effects on cognition and meditation are weaker and inconsistent. Marketing claims about “theta state” induction etc. are not well-supported. If you include binaural beats, frame as “some people find them helpful for relaxation; the research is mixed.”

**Better framing for Resonance:**

I’d reframe away from “feeling-word labels per Hz” (which implies a mapping that doesn’t exist) and toward something like:

> *“Pick the tone whose body-feel right now most matches the felt sense in your body. There’s no right answer. Sound can act as a tuning fork for what’s already there.”*

The Hz number can be a metaphor / sensory anchor without making a research claim. Honest copy: *“This is a felt-sense exercise drawing on prosody and somatic-attention research, not a frequency prescription. There’s no specific Hz that ‘does’ a thing. We’re using sound as a way to listen to your body.”*

Better lay categories you can use without overclaiming, drawn from psychoacoustic research:

- **Low (40–120 Hz):** felt as weight, grounding, density, pressure, sometimes heaviness / dorsal-feeling withdrawal
- **Mid-low (120–300 Hz):** chest resonance, voice-like, “in the body”
- **Mid (300–800 Hz):** human-voice range, social engagement, “in the room”
- **Higher (800+ Hz):** alertness, lift, sometimes activation/anxiety
  The user picks where they are; the partner sees where they are. The shared graph of “where we both are right now” is the data — not a diagnosis, an opening.

**Validated brief sound interventions for couples?** None I could find with RCT-level support specifically for couples. Music-listening interventions broadly have effect on mood (e.g., Saarikallio’s work on music in emotion regulation). Singing together activates social bonding via oxytocin and synchrony (Pearce, Launay & Dunbar, 2015, on choirs) — that’s the closest validated music-as-bonding research.

### B) WHAT I SEE — projective methods, honestly

**The honest framing:** the Rorschach as a *diagnostic instrument* has serious validity problems and you should not claim it as one. As a *clinical conversation prompt* and a *couples shared-meaning task*, ambiguous-stimulus interpretation has different (better) standing.

**The case against Rorschach as a test:**

- Wood, Nezworski, Lilienfeld & Garb’s *What’s Wrong with the Rorschach?* (2003) is the canonical critique. Many Comprehensive System scoring claims didn’t replicate; norms over-pathologized non-patients on several scales.
- The Chapmans’ classic 1969 work on “illusory correlation” with Rorschach signs of homosexuality showed how clinicians can see what they expect to see in projective interpretation.
- A 2013 *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* monograph (Mihura, Meyer, Dumitrascu, Bombel) found that some Rorschach variables have decent validity (about 13 of 65 they reviewed), most have weak or mixed support. The R-PAS (Rorschach Performance Assessment System; Meyer, Viglione et al., 2011) cleaned up some of the worst psychometric problems but the test as a whole remains contested.

**The case for ambiguous-stimulus tasks as a couples conversation tool:**

- Rorschach himself called it a “Form Interpretation Test” and warned against psychoanalytic over-interpretation. Used as a prompt for shared meaning-making rather than as a personality diagnostic, the framework is much sturdier.
- Couple co-construction of meaning around ambiguous stimuli has a small but real research literature on shared narrative and “we-talk” (Karan, Rosenthal & Robbins, 2019, found “we-talk” predicts relationship outcomes).
- Modern projective research has largely shifted to *narrative-based* methods (TAT-derived, Adult Attachment Projective; George & West, 2012) which have stronger validation than inkblots.

**Reframe recommendation.** Drop “projective psychology” framing entirely. Use *“shared meaning-making”* or *“co-construction”* — both are clinically defensible and accurate. Suggested copy:

> *“There’s no right answer to what this is. The point isn’t what we see — it’s what each of us makes of ambiguity, and what happens when we put those side by side. Therapists call this co-construction of meaning. It surfaces our internal lenses without claiming to diagnose them.”*

You could optionally add a second image after the first — Adult Attachment Projective–style scenes (silhouettes, departures, returns) tend to surface attachment-related material more reliably than inkblots, with the bonus that you can pair their interpretations with the attachment material from station #6.

Honest disclaimer to include: *“Don’t read your partner’s response as a window into their unconscious. Read it as a window into how their mind organized this specific ambiguous moment. Then ask them about it.”*

### C) SENTENCE STEMS — the validated and the high-quality

**Why incomplete-sentence tasks work:** The original research (Rohde, 1946; Sacks & Levy, 1950;  Rotter, Lah & Rafferty’s *Rotter Incomplete Sentences Blank*,   the most psychometrically validated of the lot) suggested completion tasks bypass some censorship by activating pre-conscious associative networks. Modern view: they’re better understood as semi-structured  self-disclosure prompts that elicit material more spontaneously than free-form questions. Validity is *use-dependent* — the stems must be carefully constructed for the specific question. 

A 2000 review (Holaday, Smith & Sherry, *Journal of Personality Assessment*) found sentence completion ranks among the top 5 projective methods clinicians say practitioners should be competent in.  Inter-rater reliability is the central psychometric concern.

### D) THE MIRROR — Imago, more precisely

**The full canonical Imago Couples Dialogue protocol.**

Hendrix’s classical protocol has more steps than the three-step popular version. Drawn from the Imago Clinical Training Manual:

**1. Request an appointment.** Sender asks: “Is now a good time to dialogue about ___?” Receiver agrees or proposes a near-term alternative. (This protects against being ambushed.)

**2. Send.** Sender uses “I” statements, short chunks (2–3 sentences), no character attacks. Common openers: *“Something that bothers me is…”* / *“Something I want to share with you is…”* / *“I love it when…”* / *“I feel ___ when ___.”*

**3. Mirror.** Receiver paraphrases — *not* verbatim, that’s a common misunderstanding — using “If I got that right, I think you said…” The goal is *accuracy of meaning*, not stenographic repetition. After mirroring, asks: **“Is there more?”** (This is the underrated step. Hendrix is emphatic that this prompt frequently surfaces what the sender most needed to say.) Repeat until sender says “no more.”

**4. Summary.** Receiver gives a summary of the whole send. Sender confirms or corrects.

**5. Validate.** Receiver says: *“You make sense to me. What you say makes sense because…”* This is logical validation, not agreement. The receiver locates the internal logic of the sender’s experience — given who they are, what happened to them, what they value, this experience makes sense.

**6. Empathize.** Receiver guesses the feelings: *“I imagine you might be feeling [angry, sad, scared, lonely, ashamed, hopeful…]. Is that what you’re feeling?”* Sender confirms / corrects. Receiver may also offer “I imagine that brings up [longing / fear / memory] for you” — a deeper imaginative move.

**7. Acknowledge.** Sender thanks for the dialogue. *Then* roles switch — but the new sender’s first send is in *response to* what was just said, not a new topic.

**Variations from later Hendrix work:**

- *Behavior Change Request (BCR):* after the dialogue surfaces what was hurt, the sender translates the wound into a specific, behavioral, time-bound, doable request. The receiver chooses to grant some, all, or none — this prevents the request from becoming a demand.
- *Parent-Child Dialogue:* Hendrix’s deeper work brings in the family-of-origin material — sender speaks as their wounded younger self; receiver “holds” that.
- *Appreciation Dialogue:* same protocol, but with positive content. Imago training emphasizes a 5:1 appreciation:complaint ratio.

**Research base.** Imago has weaker empirical support than EFT or Gottman methods. Hannah, Luquet & Hendrix (1997) showed pre-post improvements in 22 couples; Beeton (2006) replicated dyadic adjustment improvements among “Getting the Love You Want” workshop attendees.  There are no large RCTs. Schmidt, Luquet & Gehlert (2016, *Journal of Couple & Relationship Therapy*) showed Imago workshops improved relationship satisfaction. **Honest summary:** the dialogue protocol is widely used clinically, has small-study support, and overlaps mechanistically with the validated structures of EFT (softening) and Gottman (active listening, repair) — the *active ingredients* are well-evidenced even if Imago-as-a-whole isn’t tested at the level of those two.

**On accurate empathy — what makes it land:**

- Carl Rogers’s three conditions (1957): unconditional positive regard, congruence, accurate empathic understanding. Truax & Carkhuff operationalized “accurate empathy” on a scale; therapist-rated empathy is the single most consistent predictor of psychotherapy outcome across orientations (Elliott, Bohart, Watson & Murphy, 2018, *Psychotherapy*, meta-analysis: r ≈ .28 with outcome).
- Jamil Zaki’s distinction: cognitive empathy (understanding) + emotional empathy (resonating) + empathic concern (motivation to help). Couples often have one without the others.
- Common pitfall: receivers default to “I would have felt…” — that’s projection, not empathy. The Imago move is *guess what they feel*, then *check*.

**Common pitfalls when doing Imago without a therapist:**

1. Mirroring becomes parroting (verbatim repetition) — kills the receiver’s engagement and feels mechanical to the sender. Train: paraphrase for *meaning*.
1. Validation collapses into “you’re right” or “I agree” — that’s not validation, that’s capitulation. Validation is “given who you are, this makes sense.”
1. Empathy guess feels like reading a menu — robotic. Help users name the feeling in *their own* words and tone.
1. The receiver waits to defend instead of receiving — a common failure mode. The protocol is broken if the receiver is inwardly composing rebuttals.
1. Sender uses the structure as a permission slip to land character attacks (“I feel that you’re a narcissist”) — needs filtering.
1. The “is there more?” prompt is skipped, and the deepest material never surfaces.

**When the verdict is “not quite”:** Build a “back to mirror” path. If the sender says “almost — but the part you missed was X,” the receiver returns to mirror just that part. Don’t let “not quite” collapse the dialogue.

**Async-specific risks:** Imago was designed for in-the-room, looking-at-each-other. Async loses the prosodic safety cues. Mitigations: (a) require a brief breathing/grounding step before reading the partner’s send; (b) add a “land check” — the receiver rates their own state before responding (am I flooded? if yes, pause); (c) optional: the receiver can record an audio reflection rather than typed, which preserves prosody. Imago Workshops Inc. has cautioned that the format is meant to slow couples down — async naturally does this, which can be an asset, but only if both partners stay engaged.

## PART 3 — IMPLEMENTATION GUIDANCE

### What’s safe to do without a therapist

**Generally safe async (with good UX containment):**

- Stress-reducing conversation (external stress only)
- State of the Union (with gentle-startup scaffolding enforced)
- 36 questions
- Appreciation rituals / Find-Remind-Bind
- Love maps / Shared Meaning
- Sentence stems (curated)
- NVC structure
- Body map / interoception (with opt-out)
- Values card sort
- Imago dialogue *if* both partners are basically regulated and the topic isn’t a current rupture
- Attachment quiz (with framing as snapshot, not type)

**Requires caution / containment scaffolding:**

- IFS parts mapping (keep at identification; don’t go into unburdening)
- EFT Hold-Me-Tight content (without an EFT therapist this should be educational/exploratory, not crisis-oriented)
- Yes/No/Maybe and erotic self disclosure (not for partners with active sexual coercion or untreated betrayal trauma)
- REACH forgiveness (not for ongoing harm; not for severe attachment injuries without therapist)
- Apology architecture for severe ruptures

**Should not be DIY without a therapist:**

- Active affair work
- Trauma processing of any kind (use of EFT terminology is fine; processing the actual injury is not)
- Domestic violence — at any level. Most apps and self-help are explicitly contraindicated.
- Active addiction
- Severe mental illness in either partner

Build a “this might not be the right exercise right now” gate up front. The most important UX move is *permission to skip*. AAMFT’s general guidance: structured exercises become harmful when used to bypass a more fundamental need for stabilization or professional support.

### Common pitfalls of async vs. real-time

1. **Loss of prosody.** Text strips the very cues (tone, pace, facial expression) that polyvagal-grounded research says signal safety. Mitigations: enforce a minimum delay between sends to slow reactivity; offer audio mode; insert a grounding step before reveal.
1. **Asymmetric pacing.** One partner writes within an hour, the other waits three days. The waiter feels abandoned; the writer feels unmet. Build in “soft deadlines” with gentle nudges, and the option to choose synchronous (within 2 hours) or async (within 1 week) modes per exercise.
1. **Reading without containment.** A reveal of difficult material when one partner is at work / driving / in an argument with a colleague is harmful. Build a “ready check” — *“Do you have 15 minutes alone, somewhere private, where you can read this carefully?”* — before any reveal.
1. **The illusion of completion.** Async exercises can feel “done” without the live integration that’s where most of the change happens. Build in an explicit “talk about this in person within 24 hours” prompt.
1. **Weaponizing the framework.** Couples with high contempt can use the “official” frameworks to score points: “You’re being defensive — that’s a Four Horseman.” Explicit norm-setting against this matters.
1. **Performative completion.** Either partner gives the right-sounding answer rather than the true one. Anonymity-of-process (drafts not shared until reveal) helps. So does framing: “no one is grading this — including you.”

### Research on relationship apps

This is honestly a thinner literature than you’d hope. The most cited recent study:

- **Aicken, Gabb, Di Martino, Witney & Lucassen (2025), *JMIR mHealth and uHealth*** — mixed-methods evaluation of the Paired app (n=440 longitudinal + n=745 survey + n=20 interviews). Users with >3 months of use scored 7.03 on the Multidimensional Quality of Relationship Scale vs. 5.19 for new users (35.5% higher; p=.002). 64.3% of >1-month users agreed their relationship felt stronger.  **Caveats: self-selected sample, no control group, observational.** Gabb is on the academic side and was an external evaluator. Mechanism the authors identified: the app prompted communication that wouldn’t otherwise have happened, and the “neutral third party” framing made hard topics easier to broach. *Take the effect size with a grain of salt — but the mechanism finding is plausible and matches what couples actually report.*
- **Lasting / Talkspace Couples / Relish** — minimal independent research. Most claims are internal/marketing.
- **Gottman’s apps (Gottman Card Decks, Gottman Relationship Coach)** — built on the Gottman research base, but the apps themselves haven’t been independently RCT’d against control conditions. Roddy, Nowlan, Doss & Bradbury (2020, *J Consult Clin Psychol*) reviewed digital interventions for couples broadly and found most studied programs were therapist-supported online programs (e.g., OurRelationship — Doss et al., 2016, RCT showed significant improvements in distressed couples) rather than self-guided apps. The therapist-supported model has the strongest evidence; pure-self-guided apps are an emerging space with limited rigorous data.

**What seems to work in the small literature that exists:**

- Habit formation around small daily prompts (matches Paired data and BJ Fogg’s behavior-change work)
- Externalizing hard topics (“the app brought it up, not my partner”)
- Brief, structured, time-limited exercises beat open-ended
- Both-partners engagement matters — single-partner use predicts dropout
- Reveals/exchanges (couple-completes-together) outperform individual journaling for relationship outcomes

### Brief interventions with measurable outcomes (<10 min)

The micro-intervention literature is real but humble:

- **Finkel, Slotter, Luchies, Walton & Gross (2013), *Psychological Science*** — a 21-minute writing intervention three times over a year (writing about a recent conflict from a neutral third-party’s perspective) prevented the typical decline in marital satisfaction. Effect size was substantial. This is one of the cleanest brief-intervention findings in couples research.
- **Gable, Reis, Impett & Asher (2004), *J Pers Soc Psychol*** — “active-constructive responding” to a partner’s good news (showing genuine interest, asking questions, expressing enthusiasm) predicts relationship satisfaction more than support during bad times. 5-minute interventions training this skill have been shown to move daily satisfaction.
- **Algoe et al.** — single gratitude expressions (other-praising form) measurably increase perceived partner responsiveness next-day.
- **Aron 36 questions** — 45-minute lab procedure produces measurable closeness in strangers; replications confirm.
- **Stanley, Markman & Whitton (PREP research)** — short structured “speaker-listener” exercises (a la Imago mirroring) produce small to moderate gains in communication; longitudinal effects on satisfaction are mixed.

The honest summary: there’s a real but modest literature on brief, structured, evidence-based couple exercises that move the needle. None of it claims dramatic effects from a single 5-minute exercise; the gains come from *repeated micro-doses* — which is exactly what an app is good at.

## CLOSING NOTES FOR THE BUILD

A few framing principles I’d suggest for v2 copy:

1. **Name the framework, name the limit.** “This draws on Sue Johnson’s EFT, which has 30+ years of research; it’s not therapy and isn’t a substitute for it.” This is what makes it hold up under Tamara’s read.
1. **Default to dimensional, not categorical.** Attachment isn’t a type; horseman isn’t an identity; differentiation isn’t a level you reach. Patterns under specific conditions.
1. **Build in the off-ramp.** Every exercise should have a “this isn’t the right one for us right now” exit, and a “this brought up more than we can hold” gate to professional support. This is both ethical and de-risks the product.
1. **Where the science is weak, say so in plain language.** The Solfeggio claim, the Rorschach diagnostic claim, and the 94% Gottman claim are all places where the popular wellness-internet has badly overrun the science. A clinician will catch every overclaim. The trust dividend from being honest about uncertainty is the whole game.
1. **The strongest active ingredients across all frameworks converge on three things:**
- Slowing reactivity enough that the prefrontal cortex is back online
- Surfacing the soft / vulnerable layer beneath the protective / reactive layer
- Producing felt experience of being accurately understood by the other
  Whatever station you build, ask: is this delivering one of these three? If yes, you’re in real territory regardless of which “school” you’re nominally drawing from. If no, it’s likely decorative.

That’s what makes EFT and Gottman and Imago and IFS and NVC all *different paths to the same room.* The Lab can pull from all of them honestly so long as you keep that in view.
